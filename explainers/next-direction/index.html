Teach the engine your words

The app already writes your dictation faster than FUTO. It still makes more mistakes on names. This page explains, from zero, what the app does, where the mistakes come from, and the plan to fix them without losing speed. Each level uses only words from the levels before it.

1Level 1 · Start here

One take, from the microphone key to the text

You press the microphone key on the FUTO keyboard. Our app opens a small panel and starts to record. You speak. You press Stop. The app puts the text into the field and returns to FUTO. We call this one take.

The time between Stop and the text is the Stop wait. On FUTO it is 80 to 100 seconds, because FUTO starts to work only after Stop. On our app it is about one second, because our app works while you speak.

Measured on 10 October on six scripts, with different recordings for each app. Speed is won. Accuracy is the open question.
Goal R4: be faster and more accurate than FUTO, in English and in German. Speed is done. This page is about accuracy, with no loss of speed.
2Level 2 · Inside the model

How a speech model turns sound into words

A speech model is three parts that run one after another. The sound is first cut into frames: 10 milliseconds of audio becomes a list of 128 numbers. The encoder reads all frames of a chunk and writes a description of the speech, one line per 80 milliseconds. This is the big, slow part. The decoder remembers the last tokens it wrote. The joiner looks at one encoder line and the decoder memory, and gives a score for every possible next token.

A token is a piece of a word. The model knows 8,192 pieces, such as ▁Zür, ich, ▁the. Long or rare words are several pieces. One extra token is blank: "nothing new on this line, move on".

Our model is Parakeet TDT 0.6B v3. "0.6B" means 600 million learned numbers, called weights. Most of them are in the encoder.
Pseudocode · the simplest way to read the scores, called greedy search
# one chunk of audio; lines = encoder output
tokens = []
line = 0
while line < len(lines):
    scores, skip = joiner(lines[line], decoder.memory)
    best = argmax(scores)            # the single best token, blank included
    if best == BLANK:
        line += skip                 # TDT: the model also says how many lines to skip
    else:
        tokens.append(best)
        decoder.remember(best)      # the decoder runs again only here
text = join_pieces(tokens)

"TDT" in the model name means the model also predicts how many lines to skip. That is why it is fast: it does not stop at every line.

3Level 3 · Chunks

Why the Stop wait is short: chunks

The app does not wait for the whole take. When you pause, it closes a chunk and sends it to the model while you keep speaking. Each chunk is decoded alone; the model does not carry memory from one chunk to the next. At Stop, only the last open chunk is still waiting. The Stop wait is the time to decode that last chunk, plus any backlog.

On the CPU, decoding takes about 0.2 seconds per second of audio, so chunks 1 to 4 are done long before Stop. Any change that makes decoding slower per second makes chunk 5 slower and can create a backlog.
Rule for every idea on this page: the Stop wait must not grow. A change that makes each chunk slower is a change we cannot ship, however accurate.
4Level 4 · Files, runtime, chips

Model files, the runtime, and which chip does the work

The model is three files in the ONNX format: encoder, decoder, joiner. A program called ONNX Runtime (ORT) executes them. We do not call ORT directly. We use sherpa-onnx, a library that wraps ORT, cuts audio into frames, runs the search loop and gives us text. When the app loads the three files once, with fixed options, that is a runtime session. Many takes reuse it.

Inside ORT, an execution provider owns one kind of chip. The CPU provider is the default. The WebGPU provider reaches the phone's graphics chip. Our phone also has a TPU, a chip for AI math. On 21 October we tried the encoder on the GPU. The text was exactly the same, but each chunk took 3.5 times longer, and the Stop wait grew 4 to 6 times. The LiteRT route to the TPU failed on a 2 GiB file limit. Both are closed for now.

numbers

FP32

Each weight is a 32-bit decimal. Exact, but 4 bytes per weight: the encoder alone is about 2.4 GB in memory and slow.

numbers

INT8

Each weight is an 8-bit whole number, scaled. 4 times smaller and faster. A little precision is lost. Our shipped encoder is INT8; the decoder and joiner stay FP32.

tried

GPU / TPU

Measured: WebGPU encoder gave the same text and a 5× longer Stop wait (ADR-025). TPU numbers failed a check. Not a way forward in this window.

Everything on this page keeps the shipped top row. The plan touches only the search loop inside sherpa-onnx.
5Level 5 · Search

Greedy search, beam search, and where a word list can plug in

Greedy search is the loop in Level 2: take the best token at every step, never look back. Beam search keeps several candidate sentences at the same time, for example 4 or 8, and picks the best whole sentence at the end. Beam search is more accurate in theory, but it runs the decoder and joiner many more times per chunk.

sherpa-onnx has a feature called hotwords: a list of phrases that get a bonus score while decoding. Today sherpa-onnx applies hotwords only inside beam search. That is the whole reason the project went down the beam-search road.

The violet box is the thing we want. The amber paths are the price we paid for it. The plan moves the violet box to the teal path.

There was a second problem. With the INT8 encoder, beam search broke on one public test clip: 62 errors instead of 5. Nobody found the root cause. The workaround was the FP32 encoder, which is exact but slow and needs 3.1 GB of memory. That workaround is why the Stop wait with beam search is 2 to 2.5 seconds.

6Level 6 · The key measurement

The one measurement that sets the direction

On 12 October the project measured three variants on the same 10 recordings, on the laptop. The numbers are word errors, German then English, out of 584 and 632 words.

Source: ADR-007. The 43 listed words were all names and terms that appear in the scripts, so this shows the mechanism works. It does not show how it works on names you did not list.
The gain comes from the word list. Beam search and FP32 by themselves did not reduce English errors. From 13 to 22 October the work tried to make FP32 and beam search fast, on the GPU and the TPU. That was the wrong lever. The right lever is: make the word list work without beam search.

8Level 8 · The bet

The bet: a word list inside greedy search

Keep everything that ships today: the INT8 encoder, the CPU, greedy search. Add one thing: a phrase tree built from your word list. At each step where the model is about to emit a token, add a bonus to the tokens that continue a listed phrase. Take the best token. Move one step along the tree. That is all.

This is not our invention. NVIDIA's NeMo toolkit, which trained Parakeet, ships this exact rule for greedy search. The paper is called TurboBias. We copy the rule into sherpa-onnx, about 150 lines of C++. Upstream sherpa-onnx does not have it yet, so we also send the patch back.

The bonus only ranks tokens against each other. It never decides whether to emit at all.
Pseudocode · the greedy rule from NeMo, which the C++ patch must match byte for byte
state = tree.root
while line < len(lines):
    scores, skip = joiner(lines[line], decoder.memory)
    best = argmax(scores)                      # unboosted, blank included
    if best == BLANK:
        line += skip                           # the bonus never turns blank into a word
        continue
    for token in non_blank_tokens:
        bonus = tree.weight(state, token)      # + if it continues a phrase
                                               # − (take-back) if it breaks a partial match
                                               # 0 for every other token
        boosted[token] = scores[token] + alpha * bonus
    best = argmax(boosted)                     # over non-blank tokens only
    tokens.append(best); decoder.remember(best)
    state = tree.step(state, best)             # back to root at the end of a phrase
# with an empty list every bonus is 0, so the output equals plain greedy, byte for byte
speed

Why the Stop wait does not grow

The bonus is read only on steps that already emit a token. The encoder, decoder and joiner run exactly as often as before, also on a one-chunk take. The extra work is one tree lookup per emitted token. We check it: the joiner call count must stay within 2% of today.

accuracy

How much we can expect

In the TurboBias paper, greedy with the tree recovers 48–57% of listed phrases; beam search recovers 64–76%. So we expect a little more than half of the Level 6 gain: roughly German 45 → 40 and English 22 → 17 on the ten recordings. That alone would move us past FUTO on the six scripts.

german

What makes German harder

No listed name is a single token, and the model can split the same name in more than one way. ß and ss are different tokens. "Tensor-Chip" and "Tensor Chip" differ. The list compiler on the laptop adds every spelling form, and both given name and surname as separate entries. Never list number strings: the list had "4.80"; the model writes "4,80".

9Level 9 · The loop

A test loop that takes minutes, not days

Two weeks of history show where the time went: the phone, the measurement machinery, and accelerators. The new loop keeps the phone out until the end, and uses the laptop for everything else.

The encoder is about 45% of the work and never changes during the search. Caching it once is what makes step 2 take seconds.

Measuring small differences honestly

The Stop wait of the same build moves by up to 330 ms from run to run. A rule like "no more than 5% slower" is 33 ms and cannot be measured. So the phone visit starts with default against default: 10 recordings, 3 rounds, 30 paired differences. The spread of those differences tells us the smallest change we can see. Only then do we run default against the patch, alternated take by take. If the smallest visible change is above 100 ms, we add rounds; if it is still above 100 ms, we say "unresolved" and ship nothing.

What we stop doing

  • Frozen plans, reservations and receipts for laptop runs
  • One decision record per step
  • Reviewer agents on every patch
  • All GPU and TPU work
  • Building a laptop copy of FUTO
keep

What we keep

  • The same recordings for every variant
  • German and English reported apart
  • A no-list control for every list run
  • A phone snapshot before every visit that changes state
  • Speed numbers only from the phone
10Level 10 · Limits

What the list cannot fix, and what only you can decide

A word list helps only with words that are in the list. It does not help with names you never typed, with German compound words, with mistakes at the edge of a chunk, or with punctuation. No fix for those fits the Stop-wait budget in the next weeks. The product answer is a list you can edit yourself, in the app's settings.

There is one more hard limit. Every FUTO number we have comes from a different recording than ours. To compare the two apps on the same sound, you would play each of the 10 recordings from the laptop speaker into FUTO once and into our app twice. The two app passes tell us how noisy the replay is. That takes you 70 to 90 minutes, and it creates new recordings. The project rules currently allow no new recordings. Without this, the plan can prove that the mechanism works. It cannot prove "better than FUTO".

Before day 1, you decide:
  1. Allow laptop inference for this bet, in a draft decision record (ADR-026).
  2. Allow the replay recordings, or accept a mechanism verdict only.
  3. Decide the compound-spacing rule in the scorer. The German verdict depends on it.
  4. Read the one-hour pre-check: how many of our listed-term errors can a bonus fix at all? A spacing error cannot be fixed by a bonus.
  5. Write down which FUTO model and settings you use. FUTO recommends Multilingual-74 for German; the goal names Multilingual-244.
  6. Write a list of 30 or more real names and terms you dictate, not taken from the test scripts.
  7. Confirm you can run the native build on your machine on day 6. The agent could not build on 21 October.
  8. Decide whether new test recordings happen in this window at all (ADR-005).
11Level 11 · History

Where two weeks went

An audit of every agent transcript from 9 to 22 October, about 75 hours of main-thread work, gives this picture. The numbers are estimates from timestamps.

Only the teal bar answered the question the project exists for. Reviewer agents added about 31 hours on top; 71 of 148 helper agents were reviewers.

Tools were built where ready-made ones existed: a word-error scorer, a frozen-plan framework, a native profiler, and a full GPU integration where a one-hour benchmark of the encoder alone would have given the same answer. The plan above replaces none of them with new tools. It adds one Python file and one patch, and extends the scorer.
