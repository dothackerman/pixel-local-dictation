1Level 1 · Start here

One take, from the microphone key to the text

You press the microphone key on the FUTO keyboard. Our app opens a small panel and starts to record. You speak. You press Stop. The app puts the text into the field and returns to FUTO. We call this one take.

The time between Stop and the text is the Stop wait. On FUTO it is 80 to 100 seconds, because FUTO starts to work only after Stop. On our app it is about one second, because our app works while you speak.

Measured on 10 October on six scripts. Speed against FUTO is won. From 25 October we no longer compare with FUTO: the version you use today is the baseline.
Goal: fewer wrong words than today's app, in English and in German, at a speed you accept. The panel must appear at once (Level 10).
2Level 2 · Inside the model

How a speech model turns sound into words

A speech model is three parts that run one after another. The sound is first cut into frames: 10 milliseconds of audio becomes a list of 128 numbers. The encoder reads all frames of a chunk and writes a description of the speech, one line per 80 milliseconds. This is the big, slow part. The decoder remembers the tokens it wrote so far. The joiner looks at one encoder line and the decoder memory, and gives a score to every possible next token.

A token is a piece of a word. The model knows 8,192 pieces, such as ▁Zür, ich, ▁the. Long or rare words are several pieces. One extra token is blank: "nothing new on this line, move on".

Our model is Parakeet TDT 0.6B v3. "0.6B" means 600 million learned numbers, called weights. Most of them are in the encoder.
Pseudocode · the simplest way to read the scores, called greedy search
# one chunk of audio; lines = encoder output
tokens = []
line = 0
while line < len(lines):
    scores, skip = joiner(lines[line], decoder.memory)
    best = argmax(scores)            # the single best token, blank included
    if best == BLANK:
        line += skip                 # TDT: the model also says how many lines to skip
    else:
        tokens.append(best)
        decoder.remember(best)      # the decoder runs again only here
text = join_pieces(tokens)

"TDT" in the model name means the model also predicts how many lines to skip. That is why it is fast: it does not stop at every line.

3Level 3 · Chunks

Chunks, memory between chunks, and the Stop wait

The app does not wait for the whole take. When you pause, it closes a chunk and sends it to the model while you keep speaking. At Stop, only the last open chunk is still waiting. The Stop wait is the time to decode that last chunk, plus any backlog.

On the CPU, decoding takes about 0.2 seconds per second of audio, so chunks 1 to 4 are done long before Stop.
Your question: is the model kept warm between chunks, and could it carry memory from one chunk to the next?
Yes, it stays warm. The model files are loaded once into a runtime session, when the app starts, and every chunk and every take reuses it. What restarts at each chunk is the decoder memory. That memory is a small state (a two-layer LSTM, 640 numbers per layer) that holds the last few tokens, so the model knows that "ich" follows "▁Zür". It is not a memory of words it has heard. Carrying it across a chunk cut would help one case: a word that a pause cuts in half. It would not help a name that comes back two sentences later. For that, the right tool is a word list that grows from your corrections (Level 8). The encoder has no memory to carry: it looks at one chunk and nothing else. Carrying the decoder state is one line in the laptop test tool, so we will measure it as one row and keep it only if it helps.
Your rule from 25 October: the Stop wait may grow.
Until now every idea had to keep the Stop wait at about one second. You decided that a Stop wait of 2 to 3 seconds is acceptable if accuracy clearly improves, because the waiting can be made pleasant on screen. That reopens the most accurate variant we have ever measured as a product candidate (Level 8, candidate A).
4Level 4 · Files, runtime, chips

Model files, the runtime, and which chip does the work

The model is three files in the ONNX format: encoder, decoder, joiner. A program called ONNX Runtime (ORT) executes them. We do not call ORT directly. We use sherpa-onnx, a library that wraps ORT, cuts audio into frames, runs the search loop and gives us text. When the app loads the three files once, with fixed options, that is a runtime session. Many takes reuse it.

Inside ORT, an execution provider owns one kind of chip. The CPU provider is the default. The WebGPU provider reaches the phone's graphics chip. On 21 October we tried the encoder on the GPU. The text was exactly the same, but each chunk took 3.5 times longer. The route to the phone's AI chip (TPU) failed on a 2 GiB file limit. Both are closed.

numbers

FP32

Each weight is a 32-bit decimal. Exact, but 4 bytes per weight: the encoder alone needs about 2.4 GB in memory, and it is slower.

numbers

INT8

Each weight is an 8-bit whole number, scaled. 4 times smaller and faster. A little precision is lost. Our shipped encoder is INT8; the decoder and joiner stay FP32.

tried

GPU / TPU

Measured: the WebGPU encoder gave the same text and a 5× longer Stop wait (ADR-025). TPU numbers failed a check. Not a way forward.

Everything on this page keeps the shipped top row. The plan touches the search loop inside sherpa-onnx and the word list.
5Level 5 · Search

Greedy search, beam search, and where a word list plugs in

Your question: why is it called search, and are greedy and beam two strategies for the same thing?
Yes. At each step the joiner gives 8,193 scores, and nothing tells us the right answer. Finding the best sequence of tokens is a search through a tree of choices: every token you pick opens 8,193 new choices for the next step. Greedy search picks the best token at every step and never looks back, as in the Level 2 pseudocode. Beam search keeps several partial sentences alive at the same time, for example 4 or 8. At every step it extends each one by its best few tokens, scores all of them as whole sequences, and keeps only the best 4 or 8. At the end of the chunk it returns the best whole sequence. So it does not pick per token; it picks per sentence, with the per-token scores added up. It is more accurate when the right word starts with a token that looked slightly worse at first. The price is that the decoder and joiner run for every kept path, so 4 to 8 times more often.

sherpa-onnx has a feature called hotwords: a list of phrases that get a bonus while decoding. Today sherpa-onnx applies hotwords only inside beam search. That is the whole reason the project went down the beam-search road.

The violet box is the thing we want. The amber paths are the price we paid for it so far.
Your question: beam search broke with the INT8 encoder on one clip, and the fix was the FP32 encoder. That sounds like a weird solution.
It was a workaround, not a solution, and the note now says so. The facts: on one public 65-word clip, INT8 encoder plus beam search gave 62 wrong words; the same with the FP32 encoder gave 5. Greedy search was fine with both. So the INT8 encoder's small rounding errors do not change the single best token, but they do change the scores of the runner-up tokens, and beam search lives on those runner-up scores. Nobody found the root cause. The shipped INT8 encoder uses the simplest form of rounding (one scale for a whole weight matrix). A finer form (one scale per row) is one flag in the tool we already own and was never tried. With your 25 October rule, the FP32 encoder is acceptable for candidate A, so this does not block anything. It stays a one-day option if candidate A needs less memory or less Stop wait.
6Level 6 · The key measurement

The one measurement that sets the direction

On 12 October the project measured three variants on the same 10 recordings, on the laptop. The numbers are word errors, German then English, out of 584 and 632 words.

Source: ADR-007. The 43 listed words were all names and terms that appear in the scripts, so this shows the mechanism works. It does not show how it works on names you did not list.
The gain comes from the word list. Beam search and FP32 by themselves did not reduce English errors. From 13 to 22 October the work tried to make FP32 and beam search fast, on the GPU and the TPU. That was the wrong lever. The right lever is the word list.

8Level 8 · The plan

Two candidates: ship the measured best now, then win the speed back

candidate A · zero new code

FP32 encoder + beam 8 + your word list

Already measured: 35/11 wrong words on the 10 recordings, against 45/22 today. Stop wait 2.0 to 2.5 s on two long takes, memory 3.1 GB. With your new rule this is a product candidate. First step: one phone visit that measures its Stop wait on all 10 recordings against today's app. If the median is at or below 2.5 s, adopt it with a pleasant waiting screen.

candidate B · about 150 lines

Word list inside greedy search

The same word-list bonus, but inside greedy search, on today's INT8 encoder. One decoder and one joiner call per step, so the Stop wait stays near one second and memory stays at 2 GB. Expected: a little more than half of A's gain, roughly German 45 → 40 and English 22 → 17. B is the long-term path; A buys time.

Candidate B is not our invention. NVIDIA's NeMo toolkit, which trained Parakeet, ships this exact rule for greedy search. The paper is called TurboBias. We copy the rule into sherpa-onnx.

Your question: what does "send the patch back" mean?
sherpa-onnx is open source. Our change is a patch to its code. Today only our build has it, which means every future sherpa-onnx update forces us to re-apply it. "Sending it back" means we offer the patch to the sherpa-onnx maintainers as a pull request. If they merge it, the feature becomes part of the library, and our fork disappears. It costs us a clean patch and a short description.
The bonus only ranks tokens against each other. It never decides whether to emit at all.
Pseudocode · the greedy rule from NeMo, which the C++ patch must match byte for byte
state = tree.root
while line < len(lines):
    scores, skip = joiner(lines[line], decoder.memory)
    best = argmax(scores)                      # unboosted, blank included
    if best == BLANK:
        line += skip                           # the bonus never turns blank into a word
        continue
    for token in non_blank_tokens:
        bonus = tree.weight(state, token)      # + if it continues a phrase
                                               # − (take-back) if it breaks a partial match
                                               # 0 for every other token
        boosted[token] = scores[token] + alpha * bonus
    best = argmax(boosted)                     # over non-blank tokens only
    tokens.append(best); decoder.remember(best)
    state = tree.step(state, best)             # back to root at the end of a phrase
# with an empty list every bonus is 0, so the output equals plain greedy, byte for byte
speed

Why B keeps today's Stop wait

The bonus is read only on steps that already emit a token. The encoder, decoder and joiner run exactly as often as before, also on a one-chunk take. We check it: the joiner call count must stay within 2% of today.

accuracy

How much B can give

In the TurboBias paper, greedy with the tree recovers 48–57% of listed phrases; beam search recovers 64–76%. That paper used English models of a related kind, not ours. So the numbers above are an estimate, and the laptop tool measures the real value on day 3.

german

What makes German harder

No listed name is a single token, and the model can split the same name in more than one way. ß and ss are different tokens. The list compiler on the laptop adds both spellings and both given name and surname as separate entries, and logs every listed name the model spelled with pieces that are not in the tree.

9Level 9 · The loop

A test loop that takes minutes, not days

Two weeks of history show where the time went: the phone, the measurement machinery, and accelerators. The new loop keeps the phone out until the end, and uses the laptop for everything else.

The encoder is about 45% of the work and never changes during the search. Caching it once is what makes step 2 take seconds. Candidate A skips steps 1 to 3: it only needs step 4.

Measuring small differences honestly

The Stop wait of the same build moves by up to 330 ms from run to run. So every phone visit starts with today against today: 10 recordings, 3 rounds, 30 paired differences. The spread of those differences tells us the smallest change we can see. Only then do we run today against the candidate, alternated take by take.

10Level 10 · Responsiveness

The panel must appear at once

Your requirement from 25 October: the panel with its buttons appears slowly after the microphone key. Startup must be instant; anything the user does not need to see runs in the background.
Here is what the code does today, and why the slowness is still unexplained. The model loads on a background thread when the app's service is created, not when the panel opens. Recording starts when the panel opens and does not wait for the model: the first chunk simply queues until the model is ready. So by design the panel should never wait for the model. Yet you see it appear slowly, and that was never measured. Three suspects: Android starts our process cold when FUTO hands over; the model load uses all CPU cores at the same moment the panel is drawn and starves it; the hand-over between keyboards itself. The first step is a measurement, not a fix: time-stamp the microphone key, service start, panel shown, recording started and model ready, for ten real takes. Then fix in this order: draw the panel before any other work; load the model with lower thread priority; keep the process alive between takes.
The same waiting screen that hides a slow start also makes candidate A's longer Stop wait pleasant.
11Level 11 · Limits

What the list cannot fix, and what only you can decide

A word list helps only with words that are in the list. It does not help with names you never typed, with German compound words, or with mistakes at the edge of a chunk. No fix for those fits this window. The product answer is a list you can edit yourself, in the app's settings, that grows every time you correct a name.

Before day 1, you:
  1. Allow laptop inference and one phone visit for candidate A, in a draft decision record (ADR-026).
  2. Write a list of 30 or more real names and terms you dictate, not taken from the test scripts.
  3. Confirm you can run the native build on your machine. Candidate B needs it on day 6. The agent could not build on 21 October.
  4. Decide whether candidate A ships as soon as its phone numbers pass, before B exists.
